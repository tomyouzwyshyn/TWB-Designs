The story

The world has never spent more making ammunition. Nobody has built the industry that unmakes it.

This document is the story the deck tells — an eighty-year rearmament with no end-of-life plan, a destruction industry that exists only as scattered, ageing specialists, and a waste stream geopolitics just repriced as strategic material. The company is not a machine: it is the platform being assembled — by acquisition, by partnership, by development — into the allied world's full-service provider for munitions end-of-life. What we operate today is the foothold. The plan is the story; everything else backfills behind it.

Act I

The largest rearmament in eighty years — and it is fully priced in.

2025 closed with 65 armed conflicts involving states — the most recorded in any year since 1946. Eight of them were wars between states: the most since records began. World military expenditure reached $2.88 trillion, an all-time record — $350 for every person alive.

And the allied world is only accelerating. Europe has doubled its military spending since 2016. Rheinmetall alone is building toward 1.5 million 155mm shells a year by 2027; Washington is driving toward 100,000 a month. New ammunition plants are pouring concrete on both sides of the Atlantic.

Investors have seen this movie. Every defence index reflects it. Production is a crowded trade.

What is not priced in — anywhere — is the one property every round in that buildup shares with every round ever made: one day, someone has to unmake it. That market has no incumbent, no index, and almost no capacity.

Act II

Ammunition is the only weapon you must destroy to retire.

A warship is scrapped. A rifle sits in a rack for a century. Ammunition ages chemically: propellants destabilise, primers corrode, whole calibres are orphaned every time the gun changes. From the day it is made, every round must be guarded, inventoried and inspected — at cost, forever — until the day it is destroyed.

The arithmetic is already losing. The United States — the best-resourced military on earth — holds 303,772 short tons of munitions awaiting destruction, a queue forecast to reach 361,220 tons by 2030, spread across more than 7,000 distinct munition types. That is peacetime growth. The surge now rolling off new production lines will do to allied stockpiles what it has already done to allied budgets.

And the machinery of unmaking is where production capacity stood in 2021 — except older, dirtier, and under attack:

Burn it in a pit

The incumbent "technology" is open burning and open detonation — combustion with no containment, no emissions control. Cheap at ~$750/ton, and being regulated out of existence: the EPA's proposed rule (89 FR 19952) targets the 67 US facilities still doing it.

Truck it away

The alternative is loading live ammunition into trucks and driving it across the country to a fixed site. Every mile is a diversion window. Every transfer is a custody break. Every escort is a cost.

Do it by hand

Los Angeles County, 18 July 2025: three sheriff's detectives killed processing seized ordnance. The status quo prices human hands over live munitions — until it doesn't.

Where the lifecycle collapses into a single year, the bill arrives at once: the World Bank prices Ukraine's explosive-hazard cleanup at $30 billion — the first invoice of this war's end-of-life, not the last.

Production has been industrialised. Destruction never was. The gap between the front of the lifecycle and the back is the widest it has been in eighty years — and that gap is the company.

Act III

Then geopolitics turned the waste stream into an ore body.

Open a round of ammunition and it is mostly metal: a brass case, a copper jacket, a lead–antimony core. In November 2025, the United States placed copper, lead and antimony on its Critical Minerals List — copper and lead newly added. China had already restricted antimony exports; the price rose roughly 2,600%, past $50,000 a ton, and the Pentagon committed a reported $1 billion to secure supply.

Now read that back against the stockpiles. An allied ammunition depot is an above-ground deposit of exactly the metals the West just declared strategic — already mined, already refined, already sitting inside a guarded perimeter.

Destruction stops being a grudge purchase. Done in a closed chamber, with recovery, it is a resource business: paid once to make the liability cease to exist, paid again for the material that comes back — and the buyer's net cost of destruction falls with every ton the recovered metal offsets.

The flip, in one line

Three of the four metals a destroyed round returns — copper, lead, antimony — are now on the US Critical Minerals List. The rearmament is stockpiling our feedstock, and Washington just repriced it.

Act IV

The capability exists. The company never has.

Here is the strangest fact in this market: almost everything needed to unmake the world's munitions already exists — scattered. Artillery demilitarisation houses. Energetics and metals recovery specialists. Destruction-plant engineers. Secure-destruction operators. Dozens of firms, most founded in the 1980s and 90s, most still founder- or family-held, each holding one capability, one site, one generation of tribal knowledge.

What has never existed is the company: no platform, no consolidator at the founder scale, no single counterparty a government can hand the whole problem to. Buyers assemble their own supply chain from single-capability vendors — for the most dangerous material they own.

And the industry has never imported the technologies that transformed every adjacent sector. Robotics, autonomous handling, modern sensing, materials science — none of it has entered demilitarisation at scale. The field has not had a new idea arrive in decades.

Act V — the plan is the company

Assemble the platform: the full-service provider for munitions end-of-life.

This is not a machine business scaling a machine. It is a platform being assembled — the single counterparty an allied government calls for everything between "this must cease to exist" and "this is now material" — built on three engines running in parallel.

Acquire

Buy the back of the lifecycle while it is cheap. The firms that know how to unmake artillery, recover metals and engineer destruction plants are 30–45 years old, founder- or family-held, and standing at a succession cliff. Decades of permits, past performance and tribal knowledge — priced like plumbing at 4–7× EBITDA.

  • Metals recovery & reclamation
  • Plant & equipment engineering
  • Secure destruction — military & conflict customers
Partner

Import what the industry never had. Robotics, to take the last human hands off the most dangerous work — answering the armies' own stated objection that preprocessing exposes personnel. Universities, for sensing and materials science. Technologies from outside demilitarisation entirely — we intend to be the port they enter through.

  • Robotic handling & feed automation
  • University programs — sensing, materials
  • Cross-industry technology transfer
Develop

Build what no one sells, and own the proof. Extend our own destruction technology up the calibre ladder and into containerised and fixed-site formats — and develop the certification layer: auditable chain-of-custody destruction records, the data every regulator, treaty regime and insurer of this market will eventually require.

Act VI

The foothold — we are not assembling from a whiteboard.

TWB is already the thing most platform decks only promise: an operating business at the exact centre of the plan. eMACS — our trailer-deployed, closed-chamber destruction system for small arms ammunition to .50 calibre — has run since 2001, co-developed with the RCMP, with deployments spanning Canadian federal policing, US county law enforcement, European defence disposal and allied militaries under NDA. The same custody logic extends to seized narcotics and other seized property.

What the foothold gives the platform is more than revenue. It is the operating doctrine every acquired and developed capability will run on: same day, on site, the owner's own people; zero transfers, zero diversion windows; recovered, not released; nobody standing over live ordnance. Custody is the standard the whole catalogue inherits — and the certification data it throws off becomes the platform's proof layer.

Everyone else in this industry moves the material. We move the machine — and soon, the whole capability set. That inversion cannot be retrofitted onto a fixed-site business model, which is why the incumbents will not follow us there.

Act VII

Allied by design — sovereignty is the product.

Custody scales. The same principle that keeps a round inside a sheriff's evidence room keeps strategic material inside allied ground. In a world of 65 conflicts, no government wants its munitions — or the critical metals inside them — crossing borders to die.

The target set is the allied world: NATO, the United States, Canada, Israel, and partners. The practical standing is real and checkable: Canada is a DFARS 225.872 qualifying country — Canadian end products are exempt from Buy American in US defence acquisition — and a US entity is planned. Positioning is US-ready, allied-forward.

And the structural gift: every allied ammunition plant opening today is compounding future demand for the back of the lifecycle. Our market is being built for us, at record pace, by the largest industrial mobilisation since 1945.
